A proof has a domain #

Proposition 6.1. For every b : Bit, not(not(b)) = b.

Proof. If b is 0, the two operations give 0 → 1 → 0. If b is 1, they give 1 → 0 → 1. These are all the values of Bit. Each branch reduces to equality of identical values. □

The existing kernel checks this finite case split. Its internal constructor names are low and high. The checker runs as a separate browser module; compiling it to instructions of our small computer remains future work.

State is useful. It is not a consistency theorem.

The barber specification asks for a person who shaves exactly those people who do not shave themselves. Applying that rule to the barber requires P ↔ not P. No Boolean value satisfies it. Changing state over time can describe alternation, but that is a different specification from the original simultaneous condition.

We can avoid admitting this object by restricting formation rules. To justify such restrictions, we still need a precise language, semantics, and a soundness argument.

Gödel’s incompleteness theorems concern sufficiently expressive, effectively axiomatized arithmetic theories under consistency assumptions. Finite hardware does not invalidate them. A fixed finite domain can be exhaustively checked in principle; an extensible theory reasoning about arbitrary computations may again meet the hypotheses. A bounded proof search that stops without a proof settles neither truth nor provability.

Gödel’s original paper. Our current finite checker makes no claim to decide all arithmetic or to prove its own consistency.

The project’s question is therefore precise: which claims can a declared machine construct and check, with which resources, and under which rules?
